# Content: what goes on a slide

## Message and title
- One main message per slide, and each slide leads causally to the next.
- The title is an informative statement or a deliberate scientific question, never a topic label ("Results" or "Beyond the linear response" is wrong; "Doping the film raises the Curie temperature" is right). Do not use a logic-chain as a title.
- A question on a slide carries the purpose of the next section ("What is X and why does it generate Y?"), not just a request for a definition.

## Text rule
- Text appears only if it adds information the equation does not show, or if it is the one key message of the slide.
- Do not label what an equation obviously is ("this is the anomalous velocity").
- Do not put speaking text on the slide. The derivation is spoken or done on the board.
- Definitions that the audience may forget (e.g. the k-integral) go small, at the side.

## Equation chain
- Show the skeleton A → B → C with the key equations only; the full derivation lives in the handnote.
- Show the power of the driving variable where it matters (∝ E, ∝ E³).

## Figures act out the equations
- The picture visualizes the slide's equation chain term by term. Read the equations first, then draw each term as a physical element.
- Show the real device in 3D (e.g. a cross-shaped Hall bar on a substrate), not a flat sketch. Use several particles with trajectories, not one dot with arrows.
- Reuse equation symbols as labels (E, Ω, j_H, v_H).
- Compute before drawing: write a sign table (E, Ω, k̇, drift velocity, anomalous velocity, current) with components, draw from the table, then check each arrow in the rendered slide against it. Electrons move opposite to the current; label them e⁻.
- Do not invent the story from a generic template; infer it from the equations or ask.
- Real data (a measured curve, a paper figure) is welcome when it makes the slide denser and more convincing. Record its source; never invent or alter data.

## Claims and honesty
- Keep citations, conditions and axis definitions accurate. If the paper text was not read, say so.
- Observation is not identification (a signal alone does not prove a mechanism); say what assumptions an attribution relies on.
- Necessary is not sufficient (e.g. broken symmetry permits, does not guarantee).
- Do not present approximations as exact (e.g. a distribution correction is not a rigid shift).

## Story shape
- Experimental puzzle → mechanism → measurement → return to the same experiment and interpret it.
- Open with the paper's observation and raise the questions, but do not answer them early.
- Time and depth follow difficulty: give the hardest intuition the most room.

## Figures that show an effect, not only an equation term (2026-11-02)
- A correction term often integrates to zero and is drawn as a signed (red/blue) map. Red/blue is easily read as an unrelated binary (e.g. spin up/down) and does not show what the term does. Draw the effect instead: the original shape dashed, the changed shape solid, an arrow for the change.
- A total built from an exact solution contains all orders; do not label it as the sum of the first terms only. Use an arrow, not =, between "terms + higher orders" and the total.
- When a parameter is exaggerated for visibility, say so on the figure. Draw small displacement arrows thick.
